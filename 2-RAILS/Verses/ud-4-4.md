Verbatim:

> Yassa selūpamaṁ cittaṁ, / ṭhitaṁ nānupakampati; / Virattaṁ rajanīyesu, / kopaneyye na kuppati; / Yassevaṁ bhāvitaṁ cittaṁ, / kuto taṁ dukkhamessatī.

**Boundary ✓ — the whole verse is ONE sentence, kept whole.** It is a single
relative construction: *yassa ... cittaṁ ... kuto taṁ dukkhamessati* ("the one
whose mind is [like this] — from where would suffering come to that one?").
All six padas are quoted; nothing is cut.

**Buddhavacana gate ✓.** `Atha kho bhagavā ... imaṁ udānaṁ udānesi` — "the
Blessed One expressed this heartfelt utterance" — stands at `^ud4-4-9-1`,
directly before the verse (1-SOURCES/Text/pi-udana.md#^ud4-4-9-1). The verse is
the Buddha's own udāna.

**First-person screen ✓.** The subject is `yassa` ("the one whose"), an
indefinite relative; the closing `kuto taṁ dukkhamessati` is impersonal ("from
where would suffering come to it?"). The Buddha does not appear in any person,
and the word "Buddha" does not occur.

**⚑ Context note — the backstory is NOT needed and is deliberately excluded.**
The udāna follows a scene in which a spirit strikes Sāriputta on the head and he
barely feels it (`^ud4-4-4` ff.). That frame is vivid but the verse stands
entirely on its own: "a mind like a rock, unshaken" needs no story to land. Do
not import the striking scene into any rendering.

are annoying: from where will suffering strike one whose mind is developed like
this?"

## Disambiguated Meaning

A mind like a rock (`selūpama`), settled and not trembling (`ṭhitaṁ
nānupakampati`); dispassionate toward what would arouse craving (`virattaṁ
rajanīyesu`); not provoked by what would provoke anger (`kopaneyye na kuppati`).
The closing rhetorical question is the point: a mind developed this way — where
would suffering even come *from*?
(1-SOURCES/Translations/en-udana-sujato.md#^ud4-4-10-6) The verse is not "endure
suffering better"; it is that a steady mind removes the *handles* by which
craving and provocation get a grip at all.

**⚑ Rendering choice flagged — the two "handles" are craving and anger.**
`rajanīyesu` = things that arouse `rāga` (craving/passion); `kopaneyye` = things
that provoke `kopa` (anger). The card ships **"craving"** and **"anger"** — the
two are a matched pair (attraction and aversion), and keeping them concrete
(not "desirable things / annoying things") is what lets the reader recognise
their own experience. Flagged so a reviewer sees the pair is deliberate.

**⚑ `dukkha` ships as "suffering," rendered inside the rhetorical question.**
"Where could suffering reach it?" carries `kuto taṁ dukkhamessati` without a
gloss. The question form is load-bearing — it is what makes the card
*empowering* (nothing can get at you) rather than a flat claim; keep the
question in every rendering.

## Theme & Selection Notes

**Theme:** `steadiness-of-mind`. Related to Day 114 (`unshaken-mind`, Snp 2.4)
but a distinct angle: Day 114 is *the world's ups and downs cannot move you*
(the eight worldly conditions); this is *craving and anger cannot get a grip* —
the two inner pulls rather than outer circumstance. The rock image is fresh to
the calendar. Tag kept as `steadiness-of-mind` to mark the difference from
`unshaken-mind`.

**Card type: `simile`** — and the safe kind: the quote names its own referent
(`selūpamaṁ cittaṁ` — "the mind like a rock" *is* the thing compared), so the
reader never has to work out what the rock stands for. Clears the highest-risk
screen inside its own first pada. This keeps the type adjacency legal against
Day 132 (`observation`) and Day 134 (`teaching`, Ud 5.10, built in the same
batch).

**One-second test ✓.** "A mind like a rock — steady, unshaken by craving or
anger. Where could suffering reach it?" Read once at speed, it lands: be that
steady, and nothing gets at you.

**Not bleak, and offers a way through ✓.** The only heavy word, `dukkha`,
appears in a rhetorical question about its *absence* ("where could it reach
it?"), so the card's arc is empowerment, not diagnosis — the screen Toh 231
failed. The verse shows the steadiness *and* its result, not the trap.
