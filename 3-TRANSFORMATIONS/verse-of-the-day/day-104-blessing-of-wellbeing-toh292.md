# Day 104 — A Blessing of Wellbeing (Toh 292)

Rail: [[2-RAILS/Verses/toh292-blessing]] · own translation from the Degé Kangyur (Public Domain); 84000 English as reference only.
Short verse, **quoted in full**. Meaning: the Buddha wishing you well, hour by hour. **This card is a blessing, not a teaching.**

> **✅ GENRE APPROVED (Evan, 2026-08-03).** This card was built with an open question — all 100 preceding days had been *teachings*, and this is a benediction *received*. **Ruling: "These are both good. The problem before was the buddha praising himself. I don't want to include those, but when they are blessings and aspirations, they are good."** Blessings and aspirations are in scope; the gate is against the Buddha proclaiming his own attainment or being praised, which this is not. **No further genre decision needed.** The `སྡིག་པ` rendering ambiguity below is still open for the native reviewers.

## Source (Tibetan, Kangyur Toh 292 — verbatim)

> མཚན་མོ་བདེ་ལེགས་ཉིན་བདེ་ལེགས། །ཉི་མའི་གུང་ལ་བདེ་ལེགས་ཤིང་། །ཁྱེད་ཅག་ཀུན་དུ་བདེ་ལེགས་ལ། །ཁྱེད་ལ་སྡིག་པ་མ་འབྱུང་ཤིག (`bo-toh292.md#^p531`)

Spoken by the Blessed One to the **lay merchants of Vaiśālī** after they gave a meal to the Sangha — householders, unusually close to this product's actual audience.

No em dash ✓.
- Net: clean for review, with the genre decision and the `སྡིག་པ` ambiguity both carried forward.

### zh (Traditional)
- Back-translation: "May you be well at night, well in the daytime, and well at midday too; may you be well at all times, and may nothing bad come upon you."
- Findings: our modern Traditional rendering; the source is Tibetan, so this is fresh from the rail's meaning. 安好 used consistently for `བདེ་ལེགས`, preserving the repetition per flag (a); 吉祥 was considered and rejected as too liturgical for a plain card. 願 opens each clause, which is the natural modern Chinese optative. 臨到你身上 ships the "befall you" reading of the final line.
- Net: clean for review.

### hi
- Back-translation: "May your night be auspicious, your day be auspicious, your midday be auspicious; may you always be well, and may no misfortune come to you."
- Findings: मंगलमय for `བདེ་ལེགས` — this is the standard Indic register for *svasti* and carries the blessing genre naturally, arguably better than any other line here; the repetition is preserved per flag (a). अनिष्ट for the final line ships the "misfortune befalling you" reading.
- Confidence note: मंगलमय is warmer and more formal than English "well" — a reviewer should confirm the register is right rather than ceremonial.
- Net: clean for review.

### bo ⚑
- Back-translation (approx): "May your night be well. May your day be well. May your midday be well. May you always be well. May no harm at all come to you."
- Findings: **our own modern Tibetan rendering**, not the verbatim source. Back-translated **against the verbatim source** as rule 8 requires: all four source lines map across, with the compressed `མཚན་མོ་བདེ་ལེགས་ཉིན་བདེ་ལེགས` unpacked so each time of day takes its own verb. Modernised: `། །` breaks become separate optative clauses; the classical connective `ཤིང` dropped; `ཁྱེད་ཅག` → singular `ཁྱོད` per flag (b); the optative `ཤིག` regularised to modern `ཤོག`; and **`སྡིག་པ` rendered `གནོད་པ` ("harm")**, which commits the line to the befall-you reading.
- Confidence flags: (i) **`སྡིག་པ` → `གནོད་པ` is the substantive decision on this line.** It resolves the rail's open ambiguity in favour of "harm befalling you." If the reviewer judges the source means "may you do no wrong," this must change back to `སྡིག་པ` and the whole card's character shifts. **This is the first thing to settle.** (ii) Five `ཤོག` endings in one line — confirm this is the natural cadence for a Tibetan benediction (it may well be, as the genre is repetitive) or whether it reads mechanically. (iii) Confirm `ཉིན་གུང` is the ordinary word for midday.
