### Day 123 · 2026-10-22 — SN 1.35 (Ujjhānasaññisutta) (Pali)

- **Draft English (108 chars):** "If you will not pardon someone who owns their mistake, the anger stays in you and the grudge holds you fast."
- **Verbatim Pali:** *Accayaṁ desayantīnaṁ, / yo ce na paṭigaṇhati; / Kopantaro dosagaru, / sa veraṁ paṭimuñcati;*
- **Anchor:** `[[1-SOURCES/Text/pi-samyutta-nikaya.md#^sn1-35-11-1]]` (…`-11-4`) — verified, Sujato aligned at the same anchors.
- **Speaker:** the Buddha. He speaks stanzas 10 and 11 as one continuous utterance, closing with "I pardon your mistake," which only he can say. ⚑ **Correction to the pool's note:** stanza 10 is not "praise of the Buddha" — it is the Buddha describing himself in the *third* person. Stanza 10 must never be quoted; the card uses only stanza 11's four padas.
- **Theme:** forgiveness · **Card type:** reframe · **speaks_to:** holding a grudge you are tired of · someone apologised and you cannot let it go · quietly angry at a person you keep seeing
- **Fills the vault's oldest open gap.** `theme-checklist.md` has carried `forgiveness` as STILL OPEN since July, asking for "a standalone-intelligible verse about forgiving someone else, one that makes sense with no backstory." All four Nikāyas were grepped for *pardon/forgiv*: **every other hit is embedded in a specific confession scene.** This is the only standalone one in the Pali prose canon.
- **Dedupe:** **Day 118** (SĀ 345) is *owning your own* fault — this is the mirror, *receiving* a fault someone else has owned. **Day 106** is anger-in-general with rest as the payoff. **Day 103** (AN 5.161) is cultivating mettā toward an irritant. Nothing in Days 1–121 teaches pardon.
- **Flags:** (1) the words originate with a deity and the Buddha ratifies them — the same structure as **Day 119**; placing this at Day 123 puts four days between them, which is why it is not at Day 122. (2) The Pali is impersonal 3sg; Sujato supplies the second person — the rail must disclose this (the weaker Day-120 form, not the Day-114 form). (3) `sa veraṁ paṭimuñcati` has the person *fastening* the enmity onto himself; "the grudge holds you fast" reverses the agency, as Sujato's own "you're stuck in your enmity" does. A native reviewer will see the middle voice.
